🏗️ ARR-LargePod

A design plan for a large single-pod Cisco ACI fabric: what that topology looks like, how to size spines, leaves and the APIC cluster, which official scale limits apply inside one pod, and when to step up to Multi-Pod or Multi-Site instead.

This page complements the existing ARR — ACI Fabric Report, which is a snapshot of a specific fabric (ACI Fabric1, APIC 6.1(6g), snapshot 13 November 2026, 4:37:08 PM IST). That report is inventory and health from a read-only APIC pull. It is not a scale-out design. Use ARR to see what a fabric looks like in practice; use this page for the official design envelope of a large single pod.

Cisco also notes that these values are verified independently — you should not assume every maximum can be used at the same time in one fabric.

What a large single-pod fabric looks like

A single ACI pod is one Clos fabric: a layer of spine switches, a layer of leaf switches, and one APIC cluster managing the pod. Every leaf has a path to every spine. Endpoints, L3Outs and services attach to leaves. APICs also attach to leaves, not to spines.

In a large single pod you stay inside that one fabric and push toward the official per-pod limits, not the larger fabric-wide leaf totals that only apply when you add more pods. From the Verified Scalability Guide Table 1 [1]:

  • With 3 APICs, a pod can have up to 85 leaves (and the fabric total is also 85, so this is already a one-pod maximum).
  • With 4 APICs, or with 5 or 6 APICs, a pod can have up to 200 leaves. The fabric-wide totals of 200 (4 APICs) and 300 (5 or 6 APICs) mean a 5- or 6-node cluster can go beyond 200 leaves only by adding another pod.
  • With 7 APICs, a pod can have up to 400 leaves. The fabric-wide total of 500 leaves likewise requires Multi-Pod once you pass 400.

So a “large single pod” in current 6.2(x) verified terms is typically a 7-node APIC cluster with up to 400 leaves in that pod, or a 5- or 6-node cluster that stays at or under 200 leaves in the pod. Crossing those per-pod leaf caps is the first hard reason to go Multi-Pod [1].

Spine and leaf topology and sizing

Spines

Verified maximum is 6 spines per pod [1]. A minimum spine count is not listed in that table. Design for enough ECMP paths and for the endpoint and LPM scale of the spine hardware you pick (see endpoints below). The same guide notes that some high-scale points (for example more than 1,000 VRFs, or maximum endpoints / LPM routes) assume 32 GB RAM on the spines.

Border leaves

These are the leaves that host L3Outs toward WAN, campus or DC-core. The guide does not prescribe how many border leaves a pod must have. It does cap related objects — for example 24 border leaves per L3Out in the per-fabric table, and L3Out counts that grow with cluster size (2,400 or 10,000) [1]. Size this role from routing, bandwidth and those object limits, not from an invented ratio.

Compute leaves

These attach servers and hypervisors. Again, the official guide does not give a required count or a percentage of the pod. The binding constraint is the leaves-per-pod maximum for the APIC cluster size you choose, plus ports, oversubscription and the endpoint scale of the spines.

Service leaves

These typically front firewalls, load balancers and other service graphs. No official service-leaf count was found in the Verified Scalability Guide. Treat them as leaves that still count toward the per-pod leaf cap, and plan contracts and policy-CAM (TCAM) carefully on those switches.

Other verified topology caps from the same per-fabric table [1], independent of the Default / Medium / Large / 7-node columns: 24,000 physical ports per fabric, 20 virtual ports (vPCs) per leaf, 400 vPCs per fabric in a Layer 3 fabric (1,000 in a Layer 2 fabric), and 650 FEXs per fabric if you use them. Remote leaf is listed at 200 (100 locations with 2 remote leaves each) for a Layer 3 fabric, with a 4-remote-leaf / 2-location Multi-Pod exception in a note. If you need those designs, read that section of the guide rather than stretching this single-pod plan.

APIC cluster sizing (3, 5, 7 and standby)

Cisco documents an active cluster size of 3 to 7 APICs, and says you should size the cluster with the Verified Scalability Guide for the intended topology [3] [4] [5]. In most cases Cisco recommends a cluster of 3, 5 or 7 APICs, plus additional standby APICs. A cluster of 4 is specifically recommended for a two-site Multi-Pod fabric with between 80 and 200 leaf switches [3] [4] [5].

The Verified Scalability Guide Table 1 still publishes limits for all of those sizes — Default (3 nodes), Medium (4 nodes), Large (5 or 6 nodes) and a 7-node column [1]. Even cluster sizes are therefore documented for scale, even though the Getting Started guidance prefers odd sizes except for that Multi-Pod case.

A cold standby APIC is official in the 6.2 Getting Started guide: typically at least three active controllers plus one or more standby controllers. Switchover to a standby is a manual operation, not an automatic failover [2]. Standby controllers do not add to the Table 1 “Number of APIC nodes” used for scale. An exact official maximum number of standby APICs, or a required standby ID range, was not verified in the documents opened for this build, so those figures are not stated here.

Verified scalability limits for a single pod

Unless noted, the figures below are fabric-wide values from the same 6.2(x) guide [1]. For a single pod, use the leaves-per-pod column as the leaf cap, and remember that fabric-wide object counts (tenants, VRFs, BDs, EPGs, contracts, endpoints) are still the official published maxima for that cluster — they are not restated as a smaller “per-pod only” table in the guide.

Leaves, tenants, VRFs, bridge domains, EPGs

  • Leaves per pod: 85 (3 APICs), 200 (4, 5 or 6 APICs), 400 (7 APICs) [1].
  • Tenants: 1,000 (3 or 4 APICs) or 3,000 (5, 6 or 7 APICs) [1].
  • VRFs: 1,000 (3 or 4 APICs) or 10,000 (5, 6 or 7 APICs) [1]. High VRF counts are one of the cases where the guide assumes 32 GB spine RAM.
  • Bridge domains: 15,000 in a Layer 3 fabric, or 21,000 in a Layer 2 fabric [1]. These are in the per-fabric table (not split by cluster size).
  • EPGs: 15,000 in a Layer 3 fabric, or 21,000 in a Layer 2 fabric [1]. The guide also discusses EPGs per tenant, including special use cases; use that section if you are packing a very large tenant rather than many small ones.
  • External EPGs across all border leaves: 4,000 (3 or 4 APICs) or 10,000 (5, 6 or 7 APICs) [1].

Endpoints

Endpoint scale is not a single number. In the 6.2(x) guide it depends on spine type and on the endpoint/route scale profile (the proxy database) [1]:

  • 450,000 proxy-database entries on -EX, -FX, -GX, -GX2, -FX3, FX3-P, -H and -C spines (and the guide lists 450,000 as the maximum endpoints for those platforms in the profile discussion).
  • 360,000 on FX2 and -GX2A spines.
  • 180,000 on first-generation spines.

A single official “endpoints per pod” figure, separate from those fabric-wide proxy-database maxima, was not listed in the sections reviewed for this build. Plan endpoints against the spine generation you will actually buy, and re-read the endpoint and forwarding-scale sections of the guide for your exact SKU and profile.

Contracts and TCAM

  • Contracts per fabric: 10,000 [1].
  • Contract filters per fabric: 10,000 [1].
  • Security / policy TCAM is per leaf, not a fabric-wide pool. The per-fabric column for “Security TCAM size” is marked not applicable. The default security TCAM is 64,000 per leaf [1].
  • On 32 GB second-generation leaf switches (LSE2 / -EX/-FX and later, as named in the guide), a High Policy forwarding profile can raise policy-CAM scale up to 256,000, with compression and profile caveats that are spelled out in the guide. Do not treat 256,000 as the default [1].

How many contracts you can actually install on a given leaf depends on the leaf policy-CAM size, the forwarding profile, and how the contracts are written (filters, direction, reuse). Those engineering limits belong in the contract / forwarding-scale sections of the official guides — this page does not invent a “contracts per leaf” number beyond what the VSG states.

When to go Multi-Pod or Multi-Site instead

Stay on a single pod while the design fits the per-pod leaf cap, the building and cabling plan, and your tolerance for one fabric and one APIC cluster as a shared fault and change domain. Move when one of those no longer holds.

Choose Multi-Pod when

You need more leaves than one pod allows (200 or 400 depending on cluster size), or more than six spines in one place, or you want separate Clos fabrics in different halls or buildings under one APIC cluster [6] [7] [8] [1]. Multi-Pod keeps a single policy domain and interconnects pods over an IPN. A 5-, 6- or 7-node cluster is verified up to 25 pods and 50 spines in a Multi-Pod fabric; 3- or 4-node clusters are verified up to 6 pods [1]. A configuration error in that shared APIC cluster can still affect every pod [9] [8].

Choose Multi-Site when

You need separate APIC clusters (separate configuration and availability domains), typically across longer distances or organisational boundaries, with Nexus Dashboard Orchestrator coordinating inter-site policy [9] [8]. Cisco’s Multi-Pod vs Multi-Site overview also frames Multi-Site for geographically distributed data centres [9] [8]. Each site is still bound by that site’s APIC Verified Scalability Guide. Multi-Site scale numbers are no longer in this VSG — the 6.2(x) guide says they live in the Nexus Dashboard Orchestrator documentation. Those NDO figures were not copied here because they were not verified for this build.

This site already has an ACI Multi-Pod page if you are past the single-pod envelope.

Design checklist

  1. Pick the APIC cluster size from Table 1 for the planned leaf, tenant, VRF and L3Out scale — not only from habit. Prefer 3, 5 or 7 unless you are in the documented 4-node Multi-Pod case [3] [4] [5] [1].
  2. Stay inside the leaves-per-pod cap for that cluster (85 / 200 / 400). Do not use the larger fabric-wide leaf totals (300 or 500) as a single-pod target.
  3. Plan spines within the verified maximum of six per pod, and check spine RAM and generation against VRF, endpoint and LPM assumptions in the guide.
  4. Count every leaf role — border, compute, service, APIC-attached, and any remote or FEX designs — against the same per-pod leaf cap. Do not invent a required role split.
  5. Size contracts and filters against the 10,000 fabric-wide caps, and size policy-CAM against the per-leaf default of 64,000 or a documented High Policy profile.
  6. Size endpoints against the proxy-database limits of the actual spine SKU (450,000 / 360,000 / 180,000 as published).
  7. Add one or more cold standby APICs if you want a spare controller; remember switchover is manual [2].
  8. If you need another hall, another 200–400 leaves, or more than six spines, design Multi-Pod rather than stretching the pod.
  9. If you need a second APIC domain, geographic isolation, or NDO-managed inter-site policy, design Multi-Site and read the current NDO scalability guide.
  10. Re-read the Verified Scalability Guide for the exact APIC and switch software you will run. This page is a snapshot as of 14 Nov 2026, not a live feed.
  11. Do not assume every published maximum can be used at once. Cisco states the values are independently verified.

Risks

  • Reading fabric-wide leaf totals as a single-pod plan. 300 or 500 leaves require Multi-Pod under the 6.2(x) table.
  • Multi-dimensional scale. Hitting tenants, VRFs, EPGs, contracts and endpoints all at their published maxima in one fabric is not something the guide claims to have verified together.
  • Spine and leaf hardware mismatch. Endpoint, LPM and High Policy numbers depend on generation and RAM. Undersizing spine memory or using a mixed generation quietly lowers the ceiling.
  • Policy-CAM exhaustion on hot leaves. Border and service leaves often carry more contracts than compute leaves. The fabric can be “in scale” on contract count while an individual leaf is not.
  • One APIC cluster, one change domain. A bad policy push or cluster event is shared by the whole pod, and by every pod if you later add Multi-Pod [9] [8].
  • Cold standby is not automatic HA. A failed active APIC does not automatically promote the standby [2].
  • Even-sized clusters. 4- and 6-node sizes appear in Table 1, but general Getting Started guidance still prefers 3, 5 or 7 except for a documented Multi-Pod case. Do not pick 4 or 6 without reading that guidance.
  • Unverified role splits and NDO numbers. This page does not invent a border/compute/service ratio, a minimum spine count, a standby maximum, or Multi-Site object limits. Treat any unofficial rule of thumb as unverified until you check a current official guide.
