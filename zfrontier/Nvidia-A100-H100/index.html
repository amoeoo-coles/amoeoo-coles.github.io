🟩 Nvidia A100 & H100

A sourced snapshot of NVIDIA’s A100 (Ampere) and H100 (Hopper) Tensor Core GPUs: what each chip is, a side-by-side spec table for the main SXM, PCIe and NVL form factors, the architectural differences that matter in practice, and where the cards sit in DGX and HGX systems. Figures are taken from NVIDIA product pages, datasheets and architecture papers opened on 15 Nov 2026. Nothing here is a price or a benchmark I ran myself, and a number that NVIDIA does not publish is marked as not stated rather than guessed.

How to read the table. Tensor Core rates marked * are NVIDIA’s peak figures with structured sparsity. For A100, NVIDIA also publishes the dense rate, shown as dense / sparse*. Overview

NVIDIA A100 (Ampere)

A100 is the data-centre GPU of the NVIDIA Ampere architecture. The GA100 die is made on TSMC’s 7 nm N7 process, with 54.2 billion transistors and a die size of 826 mm². NVIDIA announced A100 and said it was in full production on 14 May 2020. The first shipping SKU used 40 GB of HBM2 at 1,555 GB/s; the 80 GB HBM2e part, with more than 2 TB/s of memory bandwidth, was announced on 16 November 2020. [3] [4] [5]

Ampere introduced third-generation Tensor Cores (including TF32 and FP64 Tensor math), structured sparsity, third-generation NVLink at 600 GB/s, PCIe Gen4, and Multi-Instance GPU (MIG), which can split one A100 into as many as seven isolated instances. The 80 GB SKUs use HBM2e and support up to seven 10 GB MIG slices. A100 does not have an FP8 Tensor format or a Transformer Engine; those arrived with Hopper. [1] [3]

The 80 GB SXM and PCIe cards share the same published peak compute rates. They differ in memory bandwidth (2,039 GB/s vs 1,935 GB/s), board power (400 W vs 300 W, with a custom-thermal SXM SKU up to 500 W), and how NVLink is delivered (native on SXM / HGX boards; a two-GPU NVLink Bridge on PCIe). [1]

NVIDIA H100 (Hopper)

H100 is the first data-centre GPU of the NVIDIA Hopper architecture. The full GH100 die is fabricated on TSMC 4N (a 4 nm-class process customised for NVIDIA), with 80 billion transistors and a die size of 814 mm². NVIDIA announced Hopper and H100 at GTC on 22 March 2022, said the GPU would be available starting in the third quarter of 2022, and announced full production on 20 September 2022. [7] [8] [9]

Hopper adds fourth-generation Tensor Cores with a Transformer Engine and FP8, fourth-generation NVLink at 900 GB/s on SXM, PCIe Gen5, DPX instructions for dynamic-programming algorithms, second-generation MIG, and Confidential Computing (a hardware trusted execution environment on the GPU). H100 SXM uses 80 GB of HBM3 at 3.35 TB/s. The earlier H100 PCIe 80 GB card used HBM2e at 2 TB/s. The later H100 NVL card is a PCIe form factor with 94 GB of HBM3 at 3.9 TB/s per GPU; two NVL cards can be paired with an NVLink Bridge for 188 GB of combined memory. [6] [8] [10]

As of 15 Nov 2026, NVIDIA’s public H100 product table highlights H100 SXM and H100 NVL. The 80 GB H100 PCIe SKU is still widely deployed and is included below from the earlier official datasheet, with that source noted on each differing row.

Key differences

  • Transformer Engine and FP8. H100’s fourth-generation Tensor Cores include a Transformer Engine and FP8 precision, which NVIDIA designed for large language-model training and inference. A100’s third-generation Tensor Cores go as far as TF32, BF16, FP16, INT8 and FP64 Tensor math — there is no FP8 path. [7]
  • Memory and bandwidth. A100 80 GB uses HBM2e, up to 2,039 GB/s on SXM. H100 SXM moves to HBM3 at 3.35 TB/s. H100 NVL is the high-memory Hopper PCIe card (94 GB HBM3, 3.9 TB/s). The earlier H100 PCIe 80 GB card stayed on HBM2e at 2 TB/s, so its memory subsystem is much closer to A100 than to H100 SXM. [1] [6]
  • NVLink 4 and scale-up. A100 uses third-generation NVLink at 600 GB/s. H100 SXM uses fourth-generation NVLink at 900 GB/s, and Hopper introduced the NVLink Switch System to connect many more GPUs at that rate. H100 PCIe and H100 NVL use a 600 GB/s NVLink Bridge between a pair of cards, not the 900 GB/s SXM fabric. [6] [7]
  • DPX instructions. Hopper adds DPX instructions aimed at dynamic-programming algorithms such as Smith–Waterman (sequence alignment) and Floyd–Warshall (routing). NVIDIA’s launch material claimed up to 7× versus A100 and up to 40× versus CPUs on those algorithms. A100 has no equivalent instruction set. [7]
  • Confidential Computing. H100 was introduced as the first NVIDIA accelerator with Confidential Computing: a hardware-based trusted execution environment that can isolate a workload on the GPU. NVIDIA does not describe this capability for A100. [6] [7]
  • MIG and power. Both generations can partition one GPU into as many as seven MIG instances (10 GB each on the 80 GB SKUs; 12 GB each on H100 NVL). Board power jumps sharply on the SXM path: 400 W (A100 SXM, CTS up to 500 W) versus up to 700 W configurable on H100 SXM. The PCIe cards stay in the 300–400 W range. [1] [6]
  • HPC double precision. A100 already added FP64 Tensor Cores (19.5 TFLOPS). H100 SXM publishes 67 TFLOPS of FP64 Tensor compute and 34 TFLOPS of non-Tensor FP64 — NVIDIA described this as tripling FP64 Tensor FLOPS versus A100. [6]

What each is best for

A100 still fits

  • Training of models that fit in 80 GB and do not need FP8 or the Transformer Engine. TF32 gives a no-code-change path for many FP32 training graphs.
  • Inference with INT8 / FP16 and MIG, when several smaller jobs should share one GPU with hardware isolation. A 10 GB MIG slice is a natural size for many older or distilled models.
  • HPC and analytics that already run well on Ampere FP64 Tensor Cores, 80 GB HBM2e and NVLink, and that do not need DPX or HBM3 bandwidth.
  • Existing DGX A100 / HGX A100 estates, and PCIe servers that were built around 300 W dual-slot cards.

H100 is the step up when

  • Training large transformers: FP8 + Transformer Engine, much higher Tensor throughput, and (on SXM) 3.35 TB/s of HBM3 and 900 GB/s NVLink. NVIDIA published up to 4× higher GPT-3 175B training versus A100 in its launch material (cluster-level, projected). [6]
  • Inference on large language models, especially where FP8 and low latency matter. NVIDIA highlighted up to 30× inference on a 530B-parameter Megatron chatbot versus A100 in the same material, and positions H100 NVL for mainstream LLM inference (for example Llama 2 70B) in power-constrained PCIe servers, with 94 GB per card and 188 GB in a bridged pair. [6]
  • HPC that wants the higher FP64 Tensor rate, HBM3 bandwidth, or DPX (genomics, routing and related dynamic-programming codes).
  • Multi-tenant or sensitive environments that need Hopper Confidential Computing or the later MIG refinements.

Those “up to 4× / 30×” figures are NVIDIA’s published comparisons, with the footnotes on the H100 product page (network, framework and model details included there). They are not measurements from this site.

Where they sit in DGX and HGX systems

NVIDIA sells both GPUs as individual PCIe cards and as SXM modules on board-level platforms:

  • DGX A100 ships eight A100 SXM GPUs in one NVIDIA system. HGX A100 is the partner board/baseboard family, published with 4, 8 or 16 GPU configurations. [1]
  • DGX H100 ships eight H100 SXM GPUs. HGX H100 is published with 4 or 8 GPU partner and NVIDIA-Certified systems. Fourth-generation NVLink (and, at larger scale, the NVLink Switch System) is the scale-up fabric inside those SXM systems. [6]
  • Hopper also underpins the Grace Hopper Superchip, which pairs a Hopper GPU with a Grace CPU over a 900 GB/s chip-to-chip interconnect. That is a different package from a standard DGX H100 SXM board, but it is the same GPU architecture. [6] [8]

Successors such as H200 and Blackwell (B200 and later) sit above this pair and are out of scope for this page.

What is uncertain or easy to misread. (1) NVIDIA’s current H100 table lists sparsity-boosted Tensor rates only; I have not invented rounded “dense” counterparts. (2) H100 NVL “188 GB” and “7.8 TB/s” figures that appear in some datasheets are aggregate for a bridged pair, not one card — this page uses the per-GPU 94 GB / 3.9 TB/s numbers from the current product page. (3) Transistor counts are for the full GA100 / GH100 dies; shipped products disable some SMs, especially H100 PCIe versus H100 SXM.
