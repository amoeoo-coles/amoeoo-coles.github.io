🔀 3DiffsVXLAN: why Cisco built ACI, SD-Access and SD-WAN differently

Cisco has three software-defined fabrics, one for the data centre, one for the campus, and one for the WAN. ACI and SD-Access both use a VXLAN overlay, but they put different policy metadata in the header and they use different control planes. SD-WAN is the odd one out: its normal data plane is IPsec or GRE, not VXLAN. This page compares the three against Cisco’s public documentation, and says where VXLAN really is — and is not — used.

The short version. The common idea is an overlay that separates a user’s identity or group from where they sit on the underlay. Only ACI and SD-Access implement that overlay with VXLAN. SD-WAN implements it with encrypted transport-layer tunnels and service VPNs. Treating all three as “VXLAN fabrics” is the usual mistake this page is here to correct.

Why three?

Each fabric grew up around a different problem, not around a shared VXLAN standard. The data centre had to move east–west application traffic at high scale, place endpoints anywhere, and enforce contracts between application groups. The campus had to follow users and devices as they roam, keep wired and wireless policy the same, and segment by identity. The WAN had to stitch branches, campuses, data centres and cloud over mixed transports, keep the overlay encrypted, and steer applications by policy.

Cisco therefore picked a control plane, a data plane and a policy model that fit each domain, then later documented ways to hand policy groups and virtual networks from one domain to the next. ISE is the usual meeting point for group identity. The overlays themselves stay different. [1] [4] [5] [9]

ACI — data centre

A spine–leaf fabric that looks like one switch to the outside, built for east–west application traffic and distributed contracts.

  • Control: COOP, IS-IS, MP-BGP
  • Data: VXLAN, with the source EPG in the header
  • Policy: EPGs or ESGs and contracts, from APIC

SD-Access — campus

A wired and wireless campus fabric that keeps a user’s identity when they move, and segments by virtual network and security group.

  • Control: LISP (EID to RLOC)
  • Data: VXLAN, with the SGT in the header
  • Policy: VNs and SGTs / SGACLs, from Catalyst Center and ISE

SD-WAN — WAN

A policy-driven WAN overlay across MPLS, internet, broadband and cellular, with centralised control and direct edge-to-edge traffic.

  • Control: OMP from SD-WAN Controllers
  • Data: IPsec or GRE between TLOCs — not VXLAN
  • Policy: service VPNs, plus optional SGT in tunnel metadata

Cisco ACI (data centre)

Domain and goals

ACI is Cisco’s application-centric data centre fabric. Official forwarding documentation describes a spine–leaf fabric that appears as a single switch to the outside, built to carry modern east–west traffic (server to server, virtual-machine mobility, multi-tier applications) without Spanning Tree blocking links. As traffic enters, ACI encapsulates it, applies policy, forwards it through a spine (a maximum of two hops), and de-encapsulates it on the way out. [1]

Control plane

Inside the fabric, ACI uses IS-IS and the Council of Oracle Protocol (COOP) for endpoint-to-endpoint forwarding. That is what lets every fabric link be active, equal-cost multipath, and fast to reconverge. To distribute routes between software-defined networks in the fabric and routers outside it, ACI uses multiprotocol BGP (MP-BGP). Spine switches can be configured as MP-BGP route reflectors. ACI also decouples an endpoint’s identity (its MAC or IP) from its location, which is a VXLAN tunnel endpoint (VTEP) on a leaf. [1]

Data plane and header

All traffic in the ACI fabric is normalised as VXLAN. At ingress, ACI encapsulates external VLAN, VXLAN or NVGRE packets into VXLAN. The official packet-format description says the ACI VXLAN header’s source group field identifies the application policy endpoint group (EPG) the packet belongs to, and the 24-bit VNID is used to forward the packet through tenant VRFs. Cisco states that a 24-bit VNID gives an address space of up to 16 million unique Layer 2 segments. Policy attributes travel in every packet so enforcement can be distributed. [1] [2]

That source group is the EPG’s policy class tag (pcTag), also called sclass or dclass depending on direction. A Cisco technical note describes pcTag as the numeric ID used inside ACI to represent an EPG for classification and contract enforcement. [3]

Engineers often call this Cisco-extended header iVXLAN. The official ACI forwarding pages opened for this article say “ACI VXLAN” and “source group field”. They do not use the name iVXLAN in the text I could read, so that name is recorded here as common usage, not as a term I found in those guides.

Segmentation and policy

ACI decouples application policy (the EPG) from forwarding. Endpoints are grouped into EPGs (tied to a bridge domain) or, in later designs, endpoint security groups (ESGs, tied to a VRF). Contracts between those groups are the policy. The pcTag is what the leaf uses when it looks up a zoning rule. pcTag values run from 1 to 65535, with documented ranges for system (1–15), global (16–16385) and local-to-VRF (16386–65535) tags. [3] [10]

Management

The fabric is managed by the Application Policy Infrastructure Controller (APIC). Policy is defined on the controller and pushed to the leaves, which enforce it in hardware as packets enter and leave the fabric.

Scale notes (only what the docs state)

Cisco documents a 24-bit VNID and “up to 16 million unique Layer 2 segments”, and a pcTag space of 1–65535 with the ranges above. I have not copied a full verified scale table (tenants, endpoints, contracts per leaf) from a data sheet, because those numbers change by platform and release. Use the scale guide for the APIC and switch release you run. [2] [3]

Cisco SD-Access (campus)

Domain and goals

Software-Defined Access is Cisco’s intent-based architecture for wired and wireless campus networks. The current design guide (Catalyst Center 2.3.7.10, LISP Pub/Sub control plane) describes it as a way to automate campus fabrics, keep security consistent whether a user is on Ethernet or Wi-Fi, and segment by identity rather than by VLAN and IP alone. [4]

Control plane

The overlay control plane is Locator/ID Separation Protocol (LISP). An endpoint’s identity (EID — MAC, IPv4 or IPv6) is mapped to a routing locator (RLOC), which in SD-Access is the Loopback 0 address of a fabric node. Fabric devices query a control-plane node (map-server and map-resolver) for that mapping, cache it, and forward to the RLOC. That split is also what lets an anycast gateway present the same subnet in many wiring closets without Spanning Tree. The design guide recommends LISP Pub/Sub for new deployments. [4]

Data plane and header

The data plane is VXLAN — a MAC-in-IP encapsulation that preserves the original Ethernet header, so the overlay can be Layer 2 or Layer 3. Each overlay network is identified by a VXLAN network identifier (VNI): a VLAN-to-VNI mapping for Layer 2, and a VRF-to-VNI mapping for Layer 3. The design guide also says SD-Access places additional information in the fabric VXLAN header — “alternative forwarding attributes” used for policy — and that in the policy plane the SGT and VRF values are encoded into that header and carried across the overlay. A 16-bit SGT is assigned by ISE in an authorisation policy. VXLAN encapsulation adds at least 50 bytes and sets the IP Don’t Fragment bit; the underlay is therefore designed with a larger MTU, commonly 9100. [4]

The design guide points to Appendix A for a bit-level diagram of the SD-Access VXLAN header. I have not transcribed that appendix here. A separate Cisco white paper on VXLAN Group Policy Option (GPO) describes carrying a 16-bit source SGT in the VXLAN header (and a Policy Applied bit). That paper is written for data-centre microsegmentation, so it is cited only as a description of the header extension, not as an SD-Access design document. [12]

Segmentation and policy

SD-Access uses two layers. Macro-segmentation is a virtual network (a VRF): complete control-plane and data-plane isolation, with inter-VN traffic only through an external device or a documented shared-services pattern such as LISP Extranet. Micro-segmentation is Cisco TrustSec: a 16-bit SGT carried in the fabric header, with group-based access control lists (SGACLs) enforced on the tags. ISE is optional if you only need virtual networks, and mandatory for identity-based micro-segmentation. ISE is the authoritative system for creating SGTs; Catalyst Center is the pane of glass for authoring and consuming them. [4]

Management

Cisco Catalyst Center owns the management plane: design, policy, provision and assurance. It automates the underlay (including LAN Automation) and the fabric roles (edge, border, control-plane, optional extended nodes and fabric wireless). A Catalyst Center outage does not, by itself, stop already-forwarding wired and wireless fabric traffic; it does stop provisioning, assurance and policy changes. [4]

Scale notes (only what the docs state)

The design guide does not put a single endpoint-count number on the public HTML page I opened. It says scale depends on the platforms, the number of endpoints and the Catalyst Center form factor, and it sends readers to the current Catalyst Center data sheet for latency and scale metrics. What the guide does state in this context: a 16-bit SGT, at least 50 bytes of VXLAN overhead, and a recommended campus-wide MTU of 9100 (with 1550 as a documented minimum to carry the overlay). [4]

Cisco Catalyst SD-WAN (WAN)

Domain and goals

Catalyst SD-WAN is a secure, policy-driven WAN that connects users, branches, data centres, clouds and SaaS over any transport, with centralised management and control. The current solution overview is explicit about the overlay: it is “a set of transport-layer tunnels, generally IPsec or GRE, managed by SD-WAN Controllers.” User traffic then flows directly between edge routers, not through the controllers. [5]

Control plane

The main control-plane protocol is the Overlay Management Protocol (OMP). It runs between SD-WAN Controllers (formerly vSmart) and WAN Edge devices over secure DTLS or TLS control connections, and distributes overlay reachability, routes, transport locators (TLOCs), policy and encryption keys. Controllers apply centralised policy and advertise the result back to the edges, which is how hub-and-spoke, full mesh or per-VPN topologies are built without configuring every edge pair. The Validator (formerly vBond) authenticates devices and helps edges and controllers find each other. SD-WAN Manager (formerly vManage) is the management system. Edges generate per-transport encryption keys and send them to the controllers, which distribute keys to peers; that avoids a full mesh of IKE negotiations. [5] [6]

Data plane and header — not VXLAN

Most data-plane connections are IPsec tunnels; GRE is also supported. The tunnels run between TLOCs on the transport side (VPN 0). BFD runs in those tunnels to measure reachability, loss, jitter and delay for application-aware routing. Segmentation is VRF-based and operationally called a VPN. The design guide says labels in OMP route attributes and in the packet encapsulation identify the VPN a packet belongs to. VPN numbers are a four-byte integer from 0 to 65535, with several values reserved; the maximum VPN you should configure is 65527. VPN 0 is the transport VPN. VPN 512 is the out-of-band management VPN, ignored by OMP and not carried across the overlay. Service-side VPNs use 1 to 65527. [5] [6]

Where VXLAN does appear in SD-WAN

VXLAN is not the Catalyst SD-WAN site-to-site data plane. It does show up in a few documented, narrower places:

  • SGT hand-off with SD-Access, not as the WAN overlay. On a two-box border/WAN-edge hand-off, the fabric border takes the SGT out of the SD-Access VXLAN header and puts it in the Cisco Meta Data (CMD) field of the Ethernet frame. The SD-WAN edge then copies that SGT into the IPsec CMD field and carries it across the SD-WAN fabric. The far edge reverses the steps, and the far border puts the SGT back into the VXLAN header. The WAN hop itself is still IPsec (or GRE), not VXLAN. Starting with 20.6.1 / 17.6.1, SGT propagation on the SD-WAN tunnel is disabled by default and must be turned on. [8] [7]
  • Inline tagging between TrustSec switches and an SD-WAN edge. Cisco document 222694 describes the same idea without a fabric: a TrustSec switch writes the SGT in an Ethernet CMD frame, the IOS XE SD-WAN edge extracts it and propagates it over IPsec or GRE, and the far edge rebuilds the CMD frame. An additional eight bytes are added for that field. [7]
  • A special multitenant / manager connector case. The Catalyst SD-WAN Security Configuration Guide for 26.x describes VXLAN connectivity for tenant-managed external services on a multitenant edge, and a VXLAN tunnel between Cisco SD-WAN Manager and an Edge Connector for each tenant. That is a management and tenant-isolation path, not the normal branch-to-branch overlay. [11]
If you remember only one fact about SD-WAN from this page: the overlay that carries user traffic between sites is IPsec or GRE. VXLAN may appear at a campus fabric hand-off, or in a documented tenant-connector design, but it is not how Catalyst SD-WAN normally builds the WAN.

Segmentation, policy and management

Service VPNs isolate business groups on the same fabric. Centralised policy on the Controllers and Manager covers topology, security, QoS and application-aware routing. Identity-based firewall policy can use SGTs learned from ISE (supported from SD-WAN 20.10.1 / IOS XE 17.10.1a in the Common Policy guide). [6] [9]

Same VXLAN idea, different problems

VXLAN’s original job is simple: take an Ethernet frame, wrap it in UDP/IP, and carry it across a routed underlay, identified by a 24-bit VNID. Both ACI and SD-Access start from that idea, then do two extra things that a plain RFC 7348 VXLAN network does not have to do.

They carry a group identifier in the header. ACI puts the source EPG (pcTag) in the source group field so a leaf can enforce contracts without looking the source up again from IP alone. SD-Access puts a 16-bit SGT in the fabric VXLAN header so a campus device can enforce TrustSec policy the same way for a wired user, a wireless user, or a user who just roamed. The mechanism is similar; the group model is not. An EPG is an application grouping inside a tenant and bridge domain (or an ESG inside a VRF). An SGT is an identity or role grouping assigned at the access edge, usually by ISE. You can map one to the other, but they are not the same object. [1] [4] [10]

They chose different control planes because the mobility problem is different. A data centre leaf needs a very fast, always-on picture of endpoints and their VTEPs — COOP on the spines, with IS-IS underneath and MP-BGP for the outside world, fits a tightly coupled fabric that is usually in one hall or a few tightly connected halls. A campus fabric needs a mapping system that can follow a laptop or a phone from one closet or AP to another without changing its IP — LISP’s EID/RLOC split and a central map-server are built for that. A WAN edge, meanwhile, has to build encrypted tunnels over transports it does not own, advertise thousands of prefixes, and change path by application. OMP plus IPsec, with controllers distributing keys and policy, is a WAN answer, not a VXLAN answer. [1] [4] [5]

Security at the WAN is a transport problem first. Campus and data-centre VXLAN usually run over a private underlay you control. A WAN overlay is expected to cross the public internet. IPsec (or GRE, where you accept the trade-off) is the natural data plane; VXLAN by itself does not give you that encryption. When Cisco wants group policy to survive the WAN hop, it copies the SGT into tunnel metadata rather than switching the WAN over to VXLAN. [5] [7] [8]

So “all based on VXLAN” is half right and half wrong. ACI and SD-Access are both VXLAN fabrics with a group tag in the header. They still differ in control plane, group model, controllers and the job they were hired to do. SD-WAN shares the idea of an overlay that carries a segment identifier — a VPN label, and sometimes an SGT — but it does not share the VXLAN encapsulation.

Interworking, only where it is documented

ACI and SD-Access (policy-group mapping through ISE)

Cisco does not merge the two VXLAN fabrics into one overlay. What is documented is a common policy path with ISE in the middle. ISE connects to APIC, learns EPGs and ESGs, and turns them into SGTs and IP-SGT bindings. In the other direction, ISE shares campus SGTs with ACI, which creates correlating external EPGs (EEPGs) on a chosen L3Out, with subnets from the SGT bindings. Contracts can be attached to those EEPGs. Campus-to-DC filtering on an SD-Access border uses SXP: ISE Policy Service Nodes share ACI-sourced bindings with the border, which can then enforce SGACLs before the traffic reaches the data centre. The current ISE/ACI workflow described by Cisco is for ISE 3.4.1 or later and ACI 6.1.1 or later. [10] [9]

The older community write-up of “SDA–ACI integration” describes the same idea (SGT ↔ EPG through ISE and pxGrid) and lists caveats that applied to that phase (for example a single ACI fabric and tenant in that design). Prefer the current ISE and Common Policy guides for what is supported now; they document multiple ACI fabrics, multi-tenant and multi-VRF, and multiple L3Outs on the current releases. [9] [10]

SD-Access and SD-WAN (VN / VPN and SGT hand-off)

The documented independent-domain design maps each SD-Access virtual network to an SD-WAN service VPN. In the two-box case, a shared 802.1Q VLAN is the mapping between a border SVI (in the VN) and a WAN-edge subinterface (in the service VPN). CTS inline tagging on both sides carries the SGT across that Ethernet link; the edge then sends the SGT across the WAN in the IPsec CMD field, as described above. The Common Policy guide summarises the pairwise integration as eBGP, VRF-lite and inline SGT between the SD-Access border and the SD-WAN edge, and says ISE can also send IP-SGT bindings into a named SD-WAN VPN. Catalyst Center can integrate with SD-WAN Manager so that fabric-specific changes and WAN-specific changes stay with their own controllers. [8] [9]

There is also a one-box, or integrated, design discussed in Cisco training and press material, where a WAN Edge takes part in both domains after Catalyst Center is API-integrated with SD-WAN Manager. The validated integration pages I opened for this article concentrate on the independent-domain (two-box) hand-off and on SGT/VPN mapping. I have not treated the one-box model as a fully quoted design here. [8]

When to use which

  • Use ACI when the problem is a data centre or colocation fabric: application groups, contracts, east–west scale, and a spine–leaf underlay you own. APIC is the controller. VXLAN is the right data plane here.
  • Use SD-Access when the problem is a campus or large branch LAN: identity, roaming, unified wired and wireless policy, and segmentation by virtual network and SGT. Catalyst Center and, for micro-segmentation, ISE are the controllers. VXLAN is again the right data plane, with LISP as the control plane.
  • Use Catalyst SD-WAN when the problem is the network between sites — including internet and cellular transports, cloud on-ramps and application-aware paths. Manager, Controllers and Validator are the control components. IPsec or GRE is the data plane. Do not expect this overlay to be VXLAN.
  • Use them together when traffic and policy have to cross domains: map SDA VNs to SD-WAN VPNs and propagate SGTs at the border; map ACI EPGs or ESGs to SGTs through ISE, and enforce at the leaf, the campus border, the WAN edge or a firewall, as the Common Policy guide describes. That is policy stitching, not one VXLAN fabric from the laptop to the server.
